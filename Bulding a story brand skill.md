# StoryBrand Messaging

This skill operationalizes the StoryBrand "SB7" method as a step-by-step working
process. The premise: people don't buy the best product, they buy the clearest
message — the one that pulls them into a story where they get something they
want. The single highest-leverage fix in almost all messaging is to stop making
the brand the hero and instead cast the **customer as the hero** and the **brand
as the guide**.

Use this skill three ways:
- **Produce** a single asset (tagline, bio, hero section, email…).
- **Audit/critique** existing copy and say exactly what to improve and why.
- **Build a full BrandScript** with the user, element by element, then derive
  every other asset from it.

Always end produced copy with a short "why this works" note tied to the
elements, so the user learns the system and can self-edit. Offer one alternative
angle whenever the framing is contestable.

---

## The core test (apply to every line)

A clear message lets someone answer three questions within seconds:
1. What do you offer?
2. How will it make my life better?
3. What do I do next to get it?

Any line that fails one of these is "noise." On a website, the reader should be
able to answer all three before scrolling.

## The two failures to hunt for first

Most weak messaging comes from one of these. When auditing, check them before
anything else:
- **The brand is the hero.** Copy centers the company's story, passion,
  credentials, or "journey." Fix: re-center on the customer; the brand appears
  only as the guide who helps.
- **The reader has to burn energy.** Too much information, jargon, cleverness, or
  no obvious next step. Fix: cut anything that doesn't move the customer's story
  forward, and make the next step unmissable.

## The seven elements, step by step

Work through these in order to build a message. For each element there are
**brainstorm questions** (to generate raw material) and a **write step** (to
choose the strongest and lock it in). For a single small asset you may compress
several elements; for a full BrandScript, do all seven.

### Element 1 — The customer (hero) wants something
Open a "story gap": name one specific thing the customer wants that your offer
helps deliver. The gap between them and that want is what holds attention.
- Pick **one** want for the overall brand. Sub-products can have their own
  secondary wants, but the top-level message stays singular — multiple competing
  wants confuse the brain and it disengages.
- The want should connect to survival/thriving in a broad sense: saving money or
  time, gaining status or belonging, finding meaning, becoming who they aspire to
  be.

Brainstorm questions:
- What does this customer most want that we can credibly help with?
- What's the gap between where they are now and that want?
- If we had to pick exactly one desire to lead with, which resonates most?

Write step: state the single want as "[Customer] wants [specific outcome]."

### Element 2 — The customer has a problem
Conflict is what makes anyone care. Define the problem on three levels, and
optionally personify it as a single "villain" (the one root cause to defeat).
- **External**: the concrete, surface problem.
- **Internal**: how the external problem makes them *feel* (frustration, doubt,
  feeling stuck, behind, not enough). This is usually the real reason people
  act — and the level most brands skip. Lead with it.
- **Philosophical**: why it's simply *wrong* that they have to deal with this
  ("you shouldn't have to…"). This gives the message moral weight.

The villain should be: a single root cause (not many), clearly negative,
recognizable, and real (no fearmongering or invented threats).

Brainstorm questions:
- What is the one "villain" — the root cause — our offer defeats?
- What's the external/tangible problem?
- What does that problem make the customer feel? (this is the internal problem)
- Why is it unjust that they face this at all? (philosophical)

Write step: choose one villain and one three-level problem.

### Element 3 — The brand is the guide
The customer is the struggling hero; the brand is the experienced guide who has
done this before. Guides win trust by showing two qualities — and only two:
- **Empathy**: prove you understand their problem, ideally that you've felt it
  too. Phrases like "We understand how it feels to…" signal this.
- **Authority/competence**: light, non-bragging proof you can actually help —
  brief testimonials, results/statistics, relevant experience, recognizable
  logos or credentials. Show enough to earn respect; not so much that it tips
  into bragging or re-centers the brand as hero.

Brainstorm questions:
- What empathy statement honestly reflects that we get their situation?
- What's the smallest, most credible proof of competence we can show?

Write step: one empathy line + one or two pieces of authority proof.

### Element 4 — The guide gives a plan
A plan removes the fear and confusion that stop people from acting. Two kinds:
- **Process plan**: 3–6 clear steps showing how to buy and/or how to use the
  offer ("1) Book a call, 2) We map your path, 3) You start"). If it needs more
  than ~6 steps, group them into phases.
- **Agreement plan**: the promises that reduce risk and build trust ("you'll
  never be pressured," "satisfaction guaranteed," "your information stays
  private"). Often expresses shared values.

Naming the plan ("The 3-Step Reinvention Path," "Our Client Promise") makes it
feel real and raises perceived value.

Brainstorm questions:
- What are the 3–6 steps to start and succeed with us?
- What fears might stop them, and what promise answers each fear?
- What could we name each plan?

Write step: one named process plan + (optionally) one named agreement plan.

### Element 5 — The guide calls them to action
People don't act unless explicitly invited. Include both kinds, always:
- **Direct call**: asks for the commitment — "Book now," "Start today,"
  "Schedule a session." Be bold; repeat it. A weak or hidden CTA reads as a
  brand that doesn't believe in its own offer.
- **Transitional call**: a low-risk next step for those not ready to buy — a free
  guide, quiz, sample, webinar, or resource. It builds the relationship, proves
  expertise, and keeps you top of mind. Think of the direct call as a proposal
  and the transitional call as a first date.

Place CTAs everywhere: site (one button top-right, repeated mid-page), emails,
bios, signatures. What feels repetitive to you is usually barely noticed by the
reader.

Brainstorm questions:
- What's the single clear direct ask?
- What free, valuable thing can we offer the not-yet-ready?

Write step: one direct CTA + one transitional CTA.

### Element 6 — Negative stakes (what they avoid)
Name what's at risk if they do nothing — the cost of inaction. Loss motivates
more strongly than gain, so this matters; but use it sparingly. Too little and
the story is boring; too much and it becomes fearmongering that repels people.
A gentle four-beat works: show they're vulnerable → say it's worth fixing →
point to the specific action → call them to it.

Brainstorm questions:
- What does the customer lose, or keep suffering, if they don't act?
- What's the lightest way to surface that without scaremongering?

Write step: up to three negative stakes (you don't need many).

### Element 7 — Positive stakes + transformation (what they gain)
Close the story gap by showing the **specific, concrete** after-state — not
"you'll be happier" but a vivid picture of life after the problem is solved
(across the external, internal, and philosophical levels). A useful tool is a
before/after grid: what they possess, how they feel, what an average day is like,
and their status — before vs. after.

Then go one level deeper to the **transformation**: the *aspirational identity*.
The strongest brands don't just sell an outcome, they help the customer become
who they want to be, and they explicitly mark the change ("you did it — you're
now ___"). Identity is the deepest motivator and the engine of word-of-mouth.

Brainstorm questions:
- Concretely, what does the customer's life look like after we help?
- Who does the customer want to *become*? How would they want others to describe
  them once they've succeeded?
- What's the "from ___ → to ___" identity shift?

Write step: top positive stakes + a "from → to" identity statement.

## The BrandScript (fill this in for the whole brand)
Once filled, derive every other asset from it. Build one for the overall brand;
optionally separate ones per service or per audience segment.

```
HERO (customer):        who they are + the one thing they want
PROBLEM
  - villain:            the single root cause to defeat
  - external:           ____
  - internal:           ____
  - philosophical:      ____
GUIDE (brand)
  - empathy line:       ____
  - authority proof:    ____
PLAN
  - process (3–6 steps, named):   ____
  - agreement (promises, named):  ____
CALL TO ACTION
  - direct:             ____
  - transitional:       ____
NEGATIVE STAKES:        up to three
POSITIVE STAKES:        the concrete after-state
TRANSFORMATION:         from ____ → to ____
```

## Implementation playbook — what a brand actually does
After the BrandScript exists, help the brand put it to work. Roughly in order:

### 1) The one-liner (logline)
One repeatable sentence combining hero + problem + plan + success. Pattern:
*"We help [hero] who [problem] to [path] so they can [success]."* Test it by
saying it aloud — if a listener asks a follow-up, it's working. Put it
everywhere: site, bios, email signatures, the team's mouths.

### 2) Overhaul the website (five highest-impact moves)
Every image, word, and idea on the site should come from the BrandScript.
- **Above the fold**: instantly show one of — an aspirational identity, a problem
  you fix, or plainly what you do — plus a visible CTA. Pass the three-question
  test before any scrolling.
- **Make the CTA unmistakable**: a distinct button, repeated (e.g. top-right and
  mid-page). Put the transitional CTA nearby but visually quieter.
- **Use images of the successful after-state** (happy customers / the
  transformation), not photos of your office or building.
- **Unify multiple offerings** under one overarching message rather than letting
  the page fragment.
- **Cut text hard**: skimmable, short, soundbites and bullets over paragraphs;
  hide detail behind "read more."

### 3) Lead generator (to grow an email list)
Offer something genuinely valuable for free in exchange for an email — not a
"newsletter." Options: a short PDF guide, a video/series, a free trial or
sample, a quiz, or a free event. It should help the customer *and* demonstrate
authority, and it doubles as a transitional CTA. Strong title patterns:
- "Common mistakes people make with [the problem]"
- "The most important things to do about [problem/goal]"
- "Become a [aspirational identity]"

### 4) Automated email campaign
A scheduled sequence whose main job is simply to keep the brand top-of-mind
(even unopened emails reinforce your name). Two email types:
- **Nurture email**: (1) raise a problem, (2) lay out a plan/insight, (3) show
  how life improves, (4) a strong P.S., (5) one-liner + contact in the
  signature. Goal: trust and reciprocity, not the hard sell.
- **Sales / call-to-action email**: (1) raise a problem, (2) name the
  product/service that fixes it, (3) show the better life, (4) a direct,
  unambiguous CTA. Don't be passive — passivity reads as low confidence.

A simple rhythm: three nurture emails, then one sales email, repeat.

### 5) Testimonials about transformation
Collect proof that shows a real before→after change, not vague praise. Guide
customers with pointed questions so the testimonial tells a mini-story:
- What problem were you facing before?
- How did that feel?
- What made working with us different?
- When did you first notice things changing?
- How is your life different now?
Then trim the questions and stitch the answers into a short narrative. Place
testimonials across the site and campaigns; they double as transitional CTAs.

### 6) Referral system
Make it easy and rewarding for happy customers to bring others. Steps: keep a
contactable database of existing customers → give them a shareable transitional
CTA (a useful video/PDF/resource) to pass on → optionally reward referrals (a
gift, discount, or affiliate cut). Automate it with your email tool, and stop the
prompts after a few orders so it never annoys.

### 7) (Optional) Internal culture
The same story aligns a team, not just customers. Share the customer BrandScript
with everyone so each role sees how it fits the story; then build an
employee-facing story where the team is the hero and leadership is the guide
(with compensation, growth, and development as the plan). Leaders, like brands,
get the outcome they want by being the guide, not the hero. The main job of
leadership is to repeat the story until everyone internalizes it.

## Guardrails / common mistakes
- **One hero, never the brand.** If the founder/company is the protagonist, fix
  that first.
- **Internal problem over external.** People act to change how they *feel*; add
  the emotional layer if it's missing.
- **Clarity beats cleverness.** A clever line that leaves the reader unsure what
  you do is worse than a plain line they grasp instantly. When unsure, cut.
- **One want, one villain, one core message.** Subplots are fine; competing main
  messages are not.
- **One CTA, repeated** — not five competing buttons.
- **Specific success, not vague success.** Paint the concrete after-state and the
  identity shift.
- **Fear is seasoning, not the meal.** Use negative stakes lightly.
- **Name your plans and lead magnets.** Naming raises perceived value.

## Language & format
- Produce output in whatever language the user is working in. For Farsi and other
  RTL languages, write idiomatically rather than translating literally — rhythm
  and naturalness matter more than word-for-word fidelity, and the one-liner
  should sound good *said aloud*.
- Match the brand's established voice and visual cues if shared (palette,
  tagline, persona). When the brand is about helping people rebuild who they are,
  keep warmth and the identity-transformation framing central.

## Worked micro-example (coaching brand)
Input: a career coach helps experienced professionals who feel their work
identity no longer fits and fear being left behind by AI.

- Hero: an experienced professional who senses their old work identity doesn't
  fit anymore.
- Problem — villain: an outdated, inherited definition of "who I am at work";
  external: skills/role feel dated; internal: quiet fear of becoming irrelevant;
  philosophical: deep experience shouldn't be discarded just because tools
  changed.
- Guide — empathy: "I know the unease of looking at your career and not
  recognizing yourself in it"; authority: years across multiple fields + client
  results.
- Plan (named "The Reinvention Path"): 1) rediscover who you are beneath the job
  title, 2) rebuild your positioning from that, 3) put AI to work for the new
  you.
- Direct CTA: book a discovery session. Transitional CTA: a free quiz, "Is your
  work identity still yours?"
- Negative stakes: another year drifting in a role that isn't you.
- Positive stakes + transformation: from *invisible and outpaced* → to *clear,
  confident, and ahead of the curve.*

One-liner: "I help experienced professionals who feel their work no longer fits
rediscover who they are and rebuild a career — and an AI edge — that's truly
theirs."
